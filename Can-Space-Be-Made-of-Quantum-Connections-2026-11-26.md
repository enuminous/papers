# Can Space Be Made of Quantum Connections?

## A field report from the EFMW quantum-gravity laboratory

*Monolithic LLC · 26 November 2026 · Popular science*

Imagine a universe before there were any places.

There is no left or right, no distance between two points, no empty space waiting for something to occupy it. There are only things that can interact and connections between them.

Now imagine that those connections obey quantum mechanics. They can change, interfere with one another and become entangled with the things they connect.

Could something resembling the space around us eventually emerge?

This is a question motivating the EFMW research program at Monolithic. Over a series of computer experiments, we have been investigating how to get from simple quantum connections to something that behaves like gravity.

We have not solved quantum gravity. But the journey has revealed some surprisingly specific reasons why the problem is hard — and what a successful explanation would need to accomplish.

## Two extraordinary theories, one unfinished conversation

Einstein's general relativity tells us that gravity is not simply a force pulling objects together. Matter and energy change the shape of spacetime, and that shape influences how matter moves.

Quantum mechanics gives us another extraordinary picture of nature. A small system can exist in a combination of different possible states. Those possibilities can interfere, and two systems can become entangled so that neither has a complete description on its own.

Both theories have passed remarkably demanding experimental tests in their respective domains. Bringing them together remains one of the great unfinished tasks of physics.

The question we are exploring is not merely whether quantum particles can move through curved space. It is whether the geometry itself might have a quantum description built from more elementary relationships.

## A universe made of connections

Our first experiments were deliberately small.

Picture four dots connected by lines. The lines tell you which dots can interact. Change the lines and you change the network through which something can travel.

In one experiment, QG-006, we allowed the network itself to participate in a quantum superposition. Instead of selecting one arrangement of lines, the mathematical model could evolve a combination of arrangements.

We tested two starting states that looked identical if you measured only which arrangement was present. The difference lay in the quantum relationship between the possibilities. As the system evolved, that initially invisible difference produced different measurable outcomes.

We also found that matter and connectivity could become entangled and influence one another within the model.

This was a small but meaningful demonstration of quantum connectivity. It was not a demonstration that those four dots constituted real spacetime. The rules were chosen by us, and the dots were labelled. The experiment gives us something simpler than the universe on which to test our ideas.

A second question followed naturally: could these connections organize themselves into something resembling three-dimensional space?

Our early rewiring experiments did not establish that they could. A network influenced by quantum energy developed more clustering than a random control, but an ordinary classical clustering algorithm did even better under the tested conditions. And none of the comparisons established a stable three-dimensional geometry arising from the dynamics.

That was a useful disappointment. A theory that can produce a convincing picture of space must do more than draw a pretty network.

## Why a quantum world does not automatically become a classical world

A quantum combination of possible networks raises another problem. Why does the world around us ordinarily appear to have a definite geometry?

We tested a mechanism called decoherence. When a quantum system interacts with its surroundings, the interference between its possible states can become increasingly difficult to observe.

In QG-007, a simulated environment monitored the arrangements of connections. The interference signal became much smaller. In some circumstances, a network that began in one arrangement was also more likely to remain there.

Yet suppressing interference did not reliably produce a single preferred network.

Think of the difference between losing the ability to distinguish the musical relationship between two notes and actually making one of the notes disappear. They are not the same process.

Decoherence is important to the transition from quantum to classical physics, but our small model did not solve the emergence of a definite classical spacetime.

## A ripple is not necessarily gravity

The next stage of research turned to gravitational waves.

A ripple can travel through a pond. Sound can travel through air. Neither is a gravitational wave.

In general relativity, a gravitational wave has two characteristic ways of stretching and squeezing the space between freely falling objects. A hypothetical quantum of such a wave is called a graviton.

Our early network models could carry waves, but their waves were not gravitons. Some had the wrong type of motion; others lacked one of the two necessary polarizations.

We added diagonal connections to give our mathematical geometry the ability to describe shear, the kind of distortion that turns a square into a parallelogram. That allowed us to represent both gravitational-wave polarization shapes.

Unfortunately, the richer model also supported four additional low-energy modes. We had given our geometry more freedom than ordinary gravity permits.

Could we simply make those unwanted motions expensive?

We tried. Increasing the energy penalties could suppress some motions, but it did not give us the exact mathematical symmetry required by gravity. Some of the extra modes remained available at sufficiently long wavelengths.

We had learned something essential: being able to represent the right shape is not the same as having the right laws of motion.

## The rules beneath the rules

General relativity contains a remarkable feature known as gauge symmetry.

A change in the coordinates used to describe a physical situation need not be a change in the physical situation itself. Two different descriptions can represent the same underlying reality.

A map can use a different grid without moving a mountain.

For gravity, this principle is encoded in a system of mathematical constraints. They determine which apparent changes in the geometry are physical and which reflect freedom in its description.

Our experiments QG-010 through QG-013 investigated whether microscopic connections could satisfy these constraints.

We constructed a precise mathematical translation between link measurements and the quantities used to describe a gravitational field. The translation worked under the assumptions of the model. But the original network's natural dynamics did not preserve the required gravitational constraints.

Even an enormous energy penalty did not turn a violation into an exact symmetry.

We encountered a further surprise in QG-013: a quantum state could remain almost entirely within its intended protected sector while its internal evolution gradually became wrong. In one exploratory simulation, leakage stayed near 0.021 percent, yet the intended polarization superposition eventually became almost orthogonal to its reference state.

Keeping the train on the tracks does not guarantee that it arrives at the right station at the right time.

That distinction matters for quantum simulations and verification far beyond this particular gravity model.

## Finding a way to carry the right waves

QG-014 and QG-015 addressed the next obstacle.

Using ideas from established tensor gauge theories and linearized general relativity, we built local lattice models with the appropriate constraints. The first construction produced two tensor polarizations but waves with the wrong relationship between frequency and wavelength.

Ordinary relativistic waves in empty space have a linear relationship between frequency and wave number. Our first gauge models instead produced quadratic or cubic behavior.

Changing the constraint architecture allowed us to construct a second model that had local interactions, preserved its linear gravitational constraints, supported two physical polarizations, and approached the correct relativistic wave behavior at long wavelengths.

We also found and removed seven spurious zero-frequency momentum points introduced by one choice of lattice derivative.

This was not a new derivation of Einstein's theory. We deliberately supplied its linearized constraint structure. The achievement was to build and test a much better reference against which a future microscopic theory can be measured.

## When matter enters the picture

A universe containing only gravitational waves would be a rather incomplete universe.

In QG-016, we introduced evolving models of matter into the lattice. An anisotropic source produced both gravitational-wave polarizations, and the corresponding constraints were preserved when the source obeyed its conservation equations.

Then we deliberately broke the conservation bookkeeping.

The gravitational waveform remained exactly the same in one control, even though the scalar gravitational constraint was violated.

That result deserves particular attention. A simulation can produce an apparently correct answer and still be wrong in a way that its headline output does not reveal. If we had checked only the waves, we would have missed the failure.

In QG-017, we closed the feedback loop in a homogeneous cosmological model. Matter influenced the expansion of space, and that expansion influenced matter. Both directions of interaction followed from a single conventional Einstein–scalar action.

All 24 complete-model experiments passed the selected checks. Removing either direction of feedback produced detectable constraint violations in all 48 matched controls.

Finally, QG-018 allowed matter and geometry to vary across space as well as time.

Imagine a spherical pulse of a mathematical field moving inward. The field changes the geometry through which it travels. That altered geometry then changes the field's subsequent motion.

We simulated precisely that reciprocal process using established equations of general relativity.

In the strongest inward pulse tested, the final matter profile differed by about 63 percent from the result obtained by artificially freezing the geometry, using the experiment's chosen relative profile norm. This is a result for a particular dimensionless simulated pulse, not a prediction of a 63 percent change in a real astronomical object.

Our first implementation failed its numerical convergence test. We kept the failure, revised the numerical method and reran the comparisons. The revised implementation passed all nine programmed checks, although some numerical errors remain. No black hole formed in the tested runs.

A failure that can be identified, documented and repaired is a productive scientific result.

## So where are we, really?

There are now two ends of a bridge.

At one end, we have small quantum systems in which the connections themselves can participate in superposition, interference and entanglement.

At the other end, we have increasingly demanding classical gravitational reference models. They carry waves, respond to conserved matter, preserve constraints, and allow geometry and matter to influence one another in a spatially varying nonlinear system.

Between these two ends, we have discovered several missing pieces.

Quantum connections do not automatically produce three-dimensional space. A tensor-shaped network does not automatically produce gravity. An energy penalty does not automatically produce gauge symmetry. And correct-looking waveforms do not automatically establish correct underlying equations.

Most importantly, the quantum graph models and the classical gravitational models are not yet one unified theory. We have not derived the latter from the former. Nor have we established a unique EFMW prediction that distinguishes it from existing physics.

The results are exploratory computational and mathematical checks, not experimental observations of quantum spacetime. Independent scientific replication remains outstanding.

## The next experiment

The next laboratory, QG-019, will investigate the boundary between quantum matter and dynamical geometry.

There is a meaningful distinction between a classical geometry responding to the average behavior of quantum matter and a geometry that is itself quantum, capable of becoming entangled with matter.

We want to understand how those different descriptions compare, which gravitational constraints each can preserve, and where they make different predictions.

Even a successful result would not complete the bridge. We would still need a defensible microscopic origin for the gravitational laws and evidence that those laws recover the behavior of the real universe.

But each experiment gives us a sharper question than the one we began with.

Science is not the process of making the universe agree with our favorite idea. It is the process of finding out which ideas survive when we make it difficult for them to be right.

For now, that is the real progress of the EFMW quantum-gravity laboratory: not a finished answer to what space is made of, but a growing collection of ways to distinguish a promising mathematical construction from a convincing imitation of one.
