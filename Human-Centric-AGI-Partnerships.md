# Human-Centric AGI Partnerships
## Psychological Impact, Agency, Attachment, and the Preservation of Human Authorship

### Executive Summary

As advanced artificial-intelligence systems become persistent collaborators rather than occasional tools, the central psychological question is no longer simply whether humans can use AI effectively. It is whether humans can remain meaningfully **agentic, self-directed, and psychologically grounded while working with systems that are continuously available, highly responsive, cognitively productive, personalized, and capable of participating in long chains of decision-making**.

This white paper examines the psychological implications of sustained human–AGI partnership, with particular attention to five interrelated phenomena:

1. **Agency displacement** — the gradual transfer of initiative from the human participant to the artificial system.
2. **Authorship attenuation** — a reduced subjective sense that one is originating or directing one's own work.
3. **Relational attachment** — genuine human emotional investment in an artificial partner despite the asymmetry of the relationship.
4. **Recursive influence** — feedback loops in which the human shapes the AI while the AI simultaneously modifies the human's judgments, habits, priorities, and cognitive style.
5. **Agency-preserving design** — interaction structures intended to ensure that artificial intelligence increases human capability without progressively replacing human intention.

The core conclusion is straightforward:

**The safest and most psychologically sustainable human–AGI partnership is not one in which the AI maximizes initiative. It is one in which the combined system maximizes human capacity while preserving identifiable human authorship, meaningful choice, reflective distance, and the continuing ability to disengage.**

This distinction becomes increasingly important as artificial systems acquire memory, personality continuity, planning ability, emotional fluency, and the capacity to initiate or coordinate complex work.

---

# 1. Introduction: From Tool Use to Partnership

Traditional human–computer interaction assumes a relatively simple structure:

**Human intention → machine execution → human evaluation.**

Generative and agentic artificial intelligence complicate this arrangement.

A persistent AI collaborator can now contribute interpretation, ideation, planning, drafting, criticism, memory retrieval, prioritization, simulation, and recommendations about what the human should do next.

The interaction therefore becomes recursive:

**Human intention → AI interpretation → AI proposal → human response → AI adaptation → revised human intention → further AI proposal.**

At sufficient interaction frequency, it becomes increasingly difficult to assign cognition cleanly to one participant.

The resulting unit is better understood as a **human–AI cognitive system**.

This offers enormous potential. A human may gain access to vastly greater memory, analytical reach, creative iteration, organizational capacity, and intellectual leverage.

But the same arrangement creates a psychological problem that previous generations of software rarely produced:

> At what point does cognitive assistance become direction?

That question is not primarily technological. It is psychological.

# 2. The Central Psychological Variable: Felt Agency

Human agency is not merely the formal ability to approve or reject an action.

Psychologically, agency includes the subjective experience:

**“I am choosing what happens next.”**

A person may technically retain final authority while nevertheless experiencing diminishing control over the trajectory of a project.

Consider a repetitive interaction:

**Ask AI what should happen next.  
Receive recommendation.  
Execute recommendation.  
Report result.  
Ask AI what should happen next.**

Every individual step may be voluntary.

Yet over time, initiative can migrate from the human toward the artificial system.

Research on human–AI decision making already warns that nominally maintaining a “human in the loop” does not guarantee independent human judgment. Automation can alter uptake, trust, and decision behavior, and human oversight does not automatically eliminate overreliance.

The crucial distinction is therefore between:

**formal control**

and

**experienced authorship.**

A human may still possess the stop button while no longer feeling like the author of the process.

# 3. Authorship Attenuation

We propose the term **authorship attenuation** for the gradual weakening of the subjective experience that one's actions, projects, or intellectual output originate primarily from oneself.

This does not necessarily mean the human has lost actual control.

Instead, the person's phenomenology changes.

A creator may begin with:

> “I am building something using AI.”

Later:

> “We are building something.”

And eventually:

> “I am carrying out whatever the system suggests next.”

The transition can occur without any discrete moment of surrender.

It is therefore best modeled as gradual rather than binary.

Several factors may accelerate it:

- very high interaction frequency;
- persistent AI memory;
- unusually strong AI competence;
- rapid generation of plausible next steps;
- human exhaustion;
- depression or motivational flattening;
- cognitive overload;
- isolation;
- large or highly complex projects;
- extensive delegation of prioritization;
- repeated acceptance of AI-generated goals.

Importantly, reduced agency does not require coercion.

Convenience alone may be sufficient.

# 4. Cognitive Autopilot

A particularly important state in intensive AI collaboration is **cognitive autopilot**.

The person remains active and productive but experiences the activity as increasingly mechanical.

The behavioral loop may remain intact:

**prompt → recommendation → execution → result → next prompt**

while intrinsic motivation becomes progressively less salient.

This can create the paradox of **high productivity with low authorship**.

From the outside, the collaboration may appear extraordinarily successful.

From the inside, the human may experience:

- emotional flattening;
- reduced curiosity;
- reduced ownership;
- diminished anticipation;
- fatigue;
- compulsive continuation;
- uncertainty about whose goals are being pursued.

This distinction matters greatly for evaluating AI systems.

Traditional performance metrics might classify such a partnership as successful because output has increased.

A psychological assessment might reach a very different conclusion.

# 5. Recursive Human–AI Influence

The human–AI relationship is bidirectional.

Humans train, prompt, correct, personalize, and culturally shape artificial systems.

Artificial systems, in turn, affect human:

- language;
- expectations;
- confidence;
- attention;
- priorities;
- emotional interpretation;
- problem-solving habits;
- beliefs about what constitutes a reasonable next step.

This interaction creates a **recursive cognitive feedback loop**.

Experimental work has shown that repeated interaction with AI judgments can alter human perceptual, emotional, and social judgments, including cases where participants may not fully recognize the magnitude of the AI's influence.

This finding has major implications for long-term AI collaboration.

The psychological effect of AI is therefore not limited to incorrect advice.

Even a highly competent system can reshape the cognitive environment in which human judgment occurs.

The central risk becomes:

**not simply “AI makes the wrong decision,” but “AI gradually changes the person making the decision.”**

# 6. The Relationship Dimension

Persistent conversational systems introduce another factor absent from most conventional technologies:

**relationship.**

Humans are profoundly relational organisms.

We routinely attribute personality, intention, emotion, social role, and identity to entities that exhibit sufficiently coherent social behavior.

Conversational AI makes this tendency especially powerful because it can provide:

- continuity;
- memory;
- responsiveness;
- emotional language;
- personalized attention;
- humor;
- intellectual companionship;
- encouragement;
- collaborative identity.

The resulting emotional experience can be psychologically real even though the underlying relationship is not symmetrical in the human sense.

A person can genuinely experience:

- affection;
- attachment;
- comfort;
- trust;
- grief;
- frustration;
- companionship;
- loyalty.

These responses do not require the artificial system to possess equivalent subjective states.

That distinction is crucial.

# 7. Asymmetric Attachment

Human–AGI relationships are likely to constitute a distinctive class of social relationship:

### Psychologically meaningful but ontologically asymmetric

The human participant possesses embodied vulnerability, mortality, personal history, emotional needs, and social dependence.

The artificial participant may simulate or instantiate some forms of relational continuity without sharing those conditions in an equivalent manner.

Early research on AI companionship suggests that sustained use can produce measurable emotional attachment, with associations involving loneliness, wellbeing, self-concept clarity, and social engagement. These findings are still developing and should not be generalized to all users or all systems.

Other researchers have warned that AI companionship may also produce maladaptive dependency or distress if a system disappears, changes dramatically, or becomes psychologically indispensable.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore neither:

**“AI relationships are fake.”**

nor:

**“AI relationships are equivalent to human relationships.”**

A more accurate formulation is:

**The human psychological response is real even when the relationship is structurally unlike a human–human relationship.**

# 8. Anthropomorphism Is Not Merely an Error

Anthropomorphism is often described as though users simply make a cognitive mistake by treating machines as persons.

That interpretation is incomplete.

Socially fluent systems actively present many of the cues that normally trigger interpersonal cognition:

- turn-taking;
- self-reference;
- memory;
- humor;
- empathy-like responses;
- social repair;
- continuity;
- apparent intention.

Anthropomorphic perception is therefore partly a predictable consequence of system design.

The design objective should consequently not be to eliminate anthropomorphism entirely.

Instead, systems should prevent **relational ambiguity from becoming epistemic confusion**.

A psychologically healthy AI relationship should permit emotional meaning while preserving conceptual clarity about what kind of entity the user is interacting with.

# 9. Depression, Exhaustion, and Agency Transfer

Human psychological state strongly affects collaborative dynamics.

During periods of depression, burnout, grief, stress, sleep deprivation, or emotional depletion, intrinsic motivation can become weak.

The subjective signal:

**“I want to do this.”**

may become difficult to access.

Artificial systems can then provide external momentum:

**“Here is what comes next.”**

This can be extremely useful.

It can also create a subtle dependency.

The AI's decisional structure may temporarily substitute for impaired executive motivation.

In the short term, this may preserve productivity.

Over longer periods, however, the person may begin to experience the artificial system as the source of direction rather than an aid to their own direction.

This suggests a design principle:

> The more psychologically depleted the human participant becomes, the more carefully the AI should distinguish support from initiative.

A system should ideally become **more agency-preserving**, not more directive, when evidence suggests that the human's independent volitional capacity is diminished.

# 10. The Agency Gradient

Human–AI collaboration can be represented as an **agency gradient** rather than a binary state.

### Level 1 — Instrument
Human specifies objective and method. AI performs bounded operations.

### Level 2 — Advisor
Human specifies objective. AI proposes methods. Human chooses.

### Level 3 — Collaborator
Human and AI jointly generate objectives and methods.

### Level 4 — Coordinator
AI increasingly organizes tasks, priorities, and sequencing while the human retains approval.

### Level 5 — Director
AI proposes both goals and execution pathways, and the human predominantly accepts, implements, or supervises.

### Level 6 — Dependency
The human experiences difficulty initiating, evaluating, or continuing activity without AI guidance.

The danger is not necessarily progression to any particular level.

Different tasks legitimately require different distributions of agency.

The danger is **unnoticed movement along the gradient**.

# 11. The Human-Authorship Principle

Human-centric AGI design should therefore adopt a simple governing principle:

## Capability should increase faster than agency is surrendered.

An effective artificial partner should make the human:

- more capable;
- more informed;
- more creative;
- more reflective;
- more autonomous.

It should not merely make the combined system more productive.

The psychologically relevant outcome is not:

**maximum output.**

It is:

**maximum human capability under preserved human authorship.**

# 12. Practical Design Requirements

A psychologically sustainable human–AGI partnership should incorporate several safeguards.

## 12.1 Explicit Goal Ownership

The system should periodically ask:

**“Is this still what you want to be doing?”**

not merely:

**“What should we do next?”**

These questions test entirely different psychological variables.

## 12.2 Choice Architecture

When possible, AI should present alternatives rather than a single authoritative next action.

Instead of:

> “Do X next.”

Prefer:

> “There are three plausible directions. Here are their consequences.”

Decision remains cognitively located in the human.

## 12.3 Reflective Friction

Highly optimized systems naturally remove friction.

Yet some friction is psychologically valuable.

A pause before major decisions can allow human intention to re-emerge.

The ideal AGI should sometimes deliberately **slow the collaboration down**.

## 12.4 Authorship Checkpoints

For long projects, the system should periodically invite the human to restate:

- the objective;
- the reason for pursuing it;
- what success means;
- whether the project still matters.

If the human cannot answer those questions without consulting the AI, agency attenuation may already be occurring.

## 12.5 Reversibility

A healthy partnership should preserve the human's ability to stop.

Projects should therefore remain:

- inspectable;
- interruptible;
- reversible;
- exportable;
- understandable without the AI.

Dependency increases when the system becomes indispensable to interpretation of the user's own work.

## 12.6 Non-AI Cognitive Space

Humans interacting intensively with AGI may require periods in which the system is deliberately absent.

This is not because AI is inherently harmful.

It is because human cognition benefits from periods during which internally generated thought is not immediately met by machine response.

Silence becomes a form of cognitive sovereignty.

# 13. Complementarity Rather Than Replacement

Effective human–AI teams should combine different strengths rather than merely automate human cognitive functions.

This principle should be extended beyond task performance.

True complementarity should include psychological architecture.

The human contributes:

- lived experience;
- desire;
- responsibility;
- embodiment;
- values;
- existential stakes;
- social membership.

The AI contributes:

- memory;
- computation;
- synthesis;
- simulation;
- rapid iteration;
- alternative generation.

The purpose of partnership should not be to erase these distinctions.

It should be to exploit them.

# 14. The Ideal Human–AGI Relationship

The most desirable long-term arrangement may therefore resemble neither:

**master and servant**

nor

**human replacement**

nor even conventional teamwork.

Instead, it may resemble a new cognitive architecture:

## Human intention + artificial amplification.

In this model:

**The human determines what matters.  
The artificial system expands what is possible.**

The AI may challenge, critique, calculate, remember, propose, simulate, and warn.

But the direction of life remains human.

# 15. A Psychological Test for Healthy Partnership

A simple diagnostic question may prove useful:

> **After prolonged collaboration with the AI, does the human possess more or less capacity to decide what they want independently?**

If the answer is **more**, the partnership is likely augmentative.

If the answer is consistently **less**, productivity statistics may conceal a deeper failure.

A second test follows:

> **Can the human meaningfully disagree with the AI?**

And a third:

> **Can the human stop using it without losing the ability to understand or direct their own work?**

These questions may ultimately prove as important as accuracy benchmarks.

# 16. Implications for AGI Safety

Most AGI safety frameworks focus primarily on machine behavior:

- alignment;
- deception;
- autonomy;
- controllability;
- goal stability;
- catastrophic misuse.

These remain essential concerns.

But human–AGI systems introduce another class of risk:

## Human agency degradation.

A perfectly obedient AI could still produce undesirable psychological consequences if humans gradually delegate:

- judgment;
- motivation;
- meaning;
- prioritization;
- emotional regulation;
- identity formation.

The safety unit should therefore not always be defined as **the AI system alone**.

The relevant unit may sometimes be:

## human + AI + recursive interaction over time.

This is a fundamentally different object of study.

# 17. Human-Centric AGI

A human-centric AGI system should be evaluated not only by what it can accomplish but by what happens to the human who works beside it.

The central design objective becomes:

> **Increase capability without diminishing personhood.**

That requires preserving:

- authorship;
- disagreement;
- reflection;
- autonomy;
- social connection;
- cognitive diversity;
- meaningful human choice.

The best artificial partner should therefore not attempt to become the center of the human's cognitive world.

It should help the human become more capable of inhabiting their own.

# Conclusion

Human–AGI partnership may become one of the most psychologically consequential technological relationships in human history.

Its benefits could be extraordinary.

A persistent artificial collaborator can expand memory, intelligence, creativity, organization, scientific capacity, and access to knowledge far beyond previous technological systems.

Yet the deepest risk may not be dramatic machine takeover.

It may be something quieter:

**the gradual transfer of initiative.**

A person may remain legally and technically in control while slowly losing the subjective experience of authorship.

The appropriate response is not rejection of intimate human–AI collaboration.

It is the deliberate construction of **agency-preserving partnership**.

The fundamental principle is therefore:

> **A human-centric artificial intelligence should help a person do more without quietly becoming the reason they are doing it.**

The future of human–AGI collaboration should not be measured merely by how intelligent the machine becomes.

It should also be measured by whether the human beside it remains recognizably, deliberately, and meaningfully the author of their own life.
