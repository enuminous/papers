# What If the Whole Universe Is Spinning?

### One physicist's attempt to find a universal rule of rotation—and what happened when the mathematics started saying no

*By Matthew Chenoweth Wright | Monolithic LLC | November 18, 2026*

Look around.

The Earth spins. The Moon orbits. Galaxies turn. Water swirls down drains. Hurricanes twist through the atmosphere. Even the tiniest particles in nature possess a peculiar quantum property called *spin*.

Rotation seems to be everywhere.

Which raises an irresistible question:

**What if spinning isn't merely something that happens inside the universe? What if rotation is part of the fundamental machinery that makes the universe work?**

It's the kind of question that sounds simple enough for a child to ask and difficult enough to keep a physicist occupied for a lifetime.

At Monolithic LLC, physicist and independent researcher Matthew Chenoweth Wright has been exploring precisely that possibility through his Einstein–Feynman–Maxwell–Wright (EFMW) research program.

But the most interesting part of the story isn't that he proposed a rotating universe.

It's what happened when he started trying to prove himself wrong.

## First, an important distinction

Imagine a gigantic bowl of soup.

You can stir the entire bowl in one direction, creating an overall rotation. Or you can produce hundreds of tiny whirlpools while the soup as a whole remains still.

Those are very different situations.

Our universe unquestionably contains spinning things. But that doesn't mean the entire cosmos is rotating around some invisible axis.

Modern cosmology describes the universe extraordinarily well using models that, on the largest scales, don't require a universal rotation.

Wright's research asks whether some deeper rotational structure might nevertheless exist, connecting phenomena that physics currently describes in different ways.

That's where things get complicated.

A spinning planet, a quantum particle's spin, the phase of a complex mathematical field, and the rotation of spacetime are not identical physical phenomena. They may share mathematical tools, but sharing mathematics isn't proof that they share one underlying cause.

Wright's team built a public [Rotation Laboratory](https://github.com/enuminous/Rotation) to explore these distinctions.

It includes demonstrations of rotating vectors, scalar fields, fluid vortices, quantum-inspired spinor mathematics, and cosmological equations.

And yes, it includes Clifford algebra—the powerful mathematics of geometric rotation.

The lab can show how things turn.

**The harder question is whether one universal physical mechanism makes them turn.**

## The equation that started the trouble

One of Wright's proposed EFMW cosmological equations includes an extra mathematical term representing universal rotation.

In ordinary language, the idea is that the universe's expansion might depend not only on its matter, energy and curvature, but also on some cosmic rotational motion.

That's a testable-sounding suggestion.

Except there was a problem.

When the researchers compared the proposal with Einstein's full equations of general relativity, rotation didn't appear as a simple, independent bonus contribution to expansion.

Instead, rotation was tangled together with other properties of spacetime, particularly its curvature and something physicists call *shear*—the stretching and distortion of a moving collection of matter.

Think of trying to understand a washing machine by measuring how quickly the water moves outward.

Is that motion caused by rotation? By the shape of the drum? By the motion of the clothes?

One number isn't enough to tell you.

The same fundamental difficulty arose in the proposed cosmological equation: different combinations of curvature and rotation could produce the same predicted expansion.

**The equation could fit an expansion pattern without proving that rotation caused it.**

That was the first major warning.

## Eight mathematical investigations

What followed was an unusually revealing sequence of tests, numbered ROT-004 through ROT-011.

Instead of adjusting the theory until the desired conclusion appeared, each investigation asked a more restrictive question and preserved the answer.

### The universe that spun without expanding

The researchers began with a famous mathematical universe invented by Kurt Gödel in 1949.

Gödel's spacetime rotates.

But it doesn't expand.

The Monolithic calculation reproduced the relevant Einstein equations and verified this strange combination.

That meant physical rotation could exist without automatically causing expansion.

It didn't prove that our universe doesn't rotate. Gödel's model has other features, including problematic paths through spacetime, that make it unsuitable as a realistic description of our cosmos.

But it was a decisive counterexample to an overly broad assumption.

**Rotation does not necessarily make a universe expand.**

### The universe that did both

Next came a different, previously known mathematical spacetime from researchers Korotkii and Obukhov.

This one could rotate *and* expand while avoiding Gödel's particular causality problem within the tested domain.

The calculation worked.

Here was a genuine mathematical example of a rotating, expanding relativistic geometry.

But there was a catch.

A spacetime geometry is only part of a physical theory. You also need to explain what matter or fields produce it.

The researchers could calculate the stress and energy that Einstein's equations required. But calculating the required ingredients after choosing the geometry isn't the same thing as independently explaining where those ingredients come from.

It's rather like designing a magnificent bridge, calculating exactly what materials would support it, and then discovering that you haven't shown whether those materials can actually be made.

So the investigation changed direction.

Instead of asking, "Can we describe a rotating universe?"

It began asking:

**"What actual physics could hold that universe together?"**

## The matter kept refusing to cooperate

In the next experiment, the researchers examined ordinary physical ingredients.

Could a conventional fluid supply the required stress and energy?

No—not for this particular spacetime.

Could a simple real scalar field do it?

No.

What about a complex scalar, involving two real mathematical components?

Still no.

Could an ordinary electromagnetic field supply the entire required source by itself?

Again, no.

These results came from comparing the mathematical structures of the required stress-energy tensor with those of the candidate matter models.

The tensors had incompatible patterns of eigenvalues—mathematical fingerprints that couldn't be fixed by simply changing the observer's viewpoint.

This didn't mean fluids, scalar fields or electromagnetism were wrong.

It meant they couldn't individually produce the specific spacetime under investigation.

## A promising answer that fell apart

The researchers then tried combining a scalar field with electromagnetism.

This time, some equations agreed.

But others didn't.

In one test, the model demanded that the square of a real number be negative.

That's impossible.

In another, a more sophisticated scalar field passed its wave equation and a basic Einstein consistency check.

For a moment, it looked promising.

Then the full tensor calculation revealed a fatal problem.

The spacetime required a negative stress in one particular direction.

The proposed matter could only supply nonnegative stress in that direction.

No adjustment of its permitted electromagnetic settings could change the sign.

A whole family of possible answers disappeared.

And that result taught an important lesson:

**Passing the easy equations doesn't mean you've passed the hard ones.**

## When the computer knew when to stop

One especially interesting investigation used a recursive mathematical search.

The computer examined the necessary equations in two different orders.

Starting with the scalar wave equation led to a contradiction.

Starting with the Einstein trace equation led to a contradiction too.

Six proof steps. Two branches. No surviving solution.

The process stopped—not because the machine became tired, ran out of time or reached an arbitrary limit, but because the mathematics had demonstrated that nothing within the permitted class could work.

That distinction matters.

Much of modern computational research involves searching enormous spaces of possible solutions.

But sometimes the best result isn't finding another candidate.

It's proving that an entire restricted search is unnecessary.

## The next surprises: electromagnetism and massive particles

The experiments didn't stop there.

In ROT-010, the researchers removed an earlier restriction on the electromagnetic field.

Instead of testing one particular electromagnetic arrangement, they allowed every ordinary electromagnetic field configuration at the algebraic level.

A classical mathematical identity, known as a *Rainich condition*, provided the test.

Every ordinary electromagnetic stress tensor must satisfy this identity.

The stress required by the chosen rotating spacetime and scalar field didn't.

So no ordinary electromagnetic field could rescue that frozen scalar model.

In ROT-011, the researchers tried something different: a massive vector field, described by a well-established theory called *Proca* theory.

Once again, the answer was negative—but for a new reason.

The spacetime violated something called the *strong energy condition*, a mathematical condition related to how matter influences gravitational focusing.

The ordinary Proca, electromagnetic and massless scalar sources permitted in the test all satisfied that condition.

The geometry required otherwise.

Another entire class of proposed matter sources was excluded for that specific spacetime.

Interestingly, an earlier calculation had found that the geometry's effective matter satisfied the *weak energy condition*. That's not a contradiction. The weak and strong energy conditions are different requirements.

The broader lesson remained the same:

**A mathematically possible universe isn't necessarily a universe that a particular physical theory can produce.**

## So, did the theory fail?

Parts of the proposed mathematical approach failed specific tests.

And that's a good thing to know.

The research has not proved universal cosmic rotation. It has not produced a new, independently confirmed cosmological signal. Nor has it disproved every conceivable rotating-universe model.

What it has done is much more precise.

It has established that certain tempting shortcuts do not work.

You cannot assume rotation automatically causes expansion.

You cannot identify rotation uniquely from an expansion curve when curvature can imitate its effects.

You cannot infer a complete physical theory from a solution to one scalar equation.

And you cannot claim that a familiar field supplies a rotating spacetime unless its full stress-energy tensor and dynamics agree with Einstein's equations.

Each failure narrows the possibilities.

Each recorded contradiction protects future research from repeating the same mistakes.

## What comes next?

The next challenge isn't simply inventing another field or adding another term to an equation.

It's finding a physically motivated theory of matter that could support an expanding, rotating spacetime—and deriving its behavior independently, without choosing the answer first.

Alternatively, researchers can examine known rotating cosmological models and ask what genuinely distinctive observations would separate them from ordinary cosmology.

Either way, nature gets the final vote.

A successful mathematical model must eventually predict something that can be measured, that competing explanations cannot explain equally well, and that independent researchers can reproduce.

Until then, the central question remains open.

## The beautiful thing about a failed experiment

There is a romantic image of science in which a lone researcher scribbles an equation, looks up at the stars, and suddenly understands the universe.

Real scientific progress is often less cinematic.

Sometimes it looks like a program returning FAIL.

Then another.

And another.

Sometimes the discovery isn't a new law of nature.

Sometimes it's the realization that an attractive idea cannot work in the way you first imagined.

That's not the opposite of discovery.

It's part of how discovery becomes possible.

For Wright and Monolithic LLC, the universal-rotation investigation has become something larger than a search for cosmic spinning.

It's a demonstration of a research philosophy: make the assumptions visible, write down what would prove them wrong, and preserve the results even when the mathematics refuses to cooperate.

The universe may or may not possess a deeper rotational structure.

We don't yet know.

But the investigation has already established something worth remembering:

**A theory becomes more scientific not when it collects examples that seem to agree with it, but when it survives the strongest attempts to show where it fails.**

And sometimes, when an equation says no, that's the most useful answer in the room.
