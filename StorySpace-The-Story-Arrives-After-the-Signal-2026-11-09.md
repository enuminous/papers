# The Story Arrives After the Signal
## StorySpace, déjà vu, and a new attempt to separate what we experience from what we think it means

*Science & Technology | November 9, 2026*

**By the Monolithic Research Desk | AI-assisted science journalism**

---

The feeling is familiar even when the scene is not.

You enter a room, hear a sentence, or turn a corner, and something inside your mind insists that the moment has already happened. Sometimes the impression is accompanied by a second conviction: you know what comes next.

For a few seconds, ordinary time seems to have developed a seam.

The experience has a name—*déjà vu*—but the name does not explain it. Neither does its intensity. An overwhelmingly convincing feeling can still be a mistaken interpretation of perfectly ordinary information.

That tension, between the authenticity of an experience and the uncertainty of its explanation, is the starting point for **StorySpace**, a new public research repository and local-first observatory developed under Matthew Chenoweth Wright's Monolithic LLC research program.

Its central proposition fits on a single line:

**The signal is real. The story can be wrong.**

StorySpace does not claim to have discovered human precognition or evidence that information flows backward through time. It is an attempt to create the documentation, controls, and experimental discipline that would be needed before treating such an explanation as science.

That sounds modest. In a world of instant grand theories and AI-generated certainty, it is also surprisingly ambitious.

## I. The conversation that started it

The project began in an online exchange about retrocausality—the idea, broadly construed, that future boundary conditions or events might play a role in the explanation of earlier physical events.

The discussion passed through familiar territory: time-symmetric equations, advanced-wave solutions, the transactional interpretation of quantum mechanics, two-state-vector formalisms, and delayed-choice experiments.

One participant answered a different question, about time dilation. Another criticized the answer and later acknowledged having generalized too quickly. Further corrections followed.

The resulting exchange was less interested in winning an argument than in keeping distinct questions from collapsing into one another.

Does a mathematical formalism admit a future-directed boundary condition? Yes, certain formalisms do.

Does a delayed-choice experiment yield surprising correlations? Yes.

Does that demonstrate that an observer can send a controllable message into yesterday? No such operational demonstration follows.

Then the discussion turned from physics to ordinary human experience.

Wright brought up déjà vu: those brief episodes in which time feels less sequential than it ought to. A participant identified a familiar cognitive possibility: a sense of recognition may appear without access to the particular memory that would ordinarily justify it.

Wright supplied the word that changed the direction of the conversation:

**Storyspace.**

It described the layer that appears when human beings take a sensation and build a narrative around it. The report of familiarity comes first; the interpretation may arrive afterward and feel, from the inside, inseparable from the original experience.

Boardy, a name used by one interlocutor whose comments Wright relayed, put it this way:

> "The story arrives after the signal and feels like part of it."

That is not a quotation from a peer-reviewed experiment, and the discussion was not independent scientific review. But it is a clear description of the problem StorySpace was built to investigate.

The difficulty is not necessarily that people lie or invent experiences. It is that **feeling, memory, explanation, and confidence can arrive in consciousness as one apparent package**, even when they have different evidentiary status.

## II. What neuroscience can tell us

Scientists have studied déjà vu as a possible disturbance or conflict in recognition and familiarity processing.

One line of research asks whether a current scene can resemble a previously encountered scene closely enough to produce a sense of familiarity—even when the person cannot consciously retrieve the earlier scene.

In a 2009 study published in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, Anne Cleary, Anthony Ryals, and Jason Nomi investigated that possibility with scene-similarity methods. Their findings provided support for the idea that resemblance to an unrecalled prior experience can produce familiarity and déjà vu reports.

But the more pointed experiment for StorySpace appeared in 2018.

Cleary and Alexander Claxton published **"Déjà Vu: An Illusion of Prediction"** in *Psychological Science*. The researchers asked whether people experiencing experimentally induced déjà vu could actually anticipate the next turn in a navigational path resembling a previously seen but unrecalled route.

They found an important split: participants reported a stronger **feeling** that they knew which turn would come next, but their next-turn predictions did not exceed chance performance in that tested task.

The sense of foresight was real as a report of experience. The extra predictive ability was not demonstrated.

That result does not explain every episode of déjà vu. It does not license dismissing anyone's experience, and it is not a universal proof against unusual predictive processes.

It does show why *how it felt* and *what it predicted* must be recorded separately.

StorySpace tries to make that distinction operational rather than rhetorical.

## III. Three spaces, three different questions

The repository organizes its observatory around three deliberately separate layers.

**Signalspace** is the experience itself.

A user can describe a moment of déjà vu, a sudden impression of familiarity, a dream that seems to resemble something later encountered, or another unusual temporal sensation. The record does not require the person to endorse any particular explanation.

**Storyspace** is the meaning constructed around that experience.

Perhaps the sensation came from resemblance to a forgotten scene. Perhaps it was an intuitive forecast based on information the person had not consciously recognized. Perhaps it was coincidence or selective memory. Perhaps someone wants to entertain a more speculative explanation.

Those possibilities are treated as interpretations—not as observations simply because they feel convincing.

**Evidencespace** asks what could distinguish the interpretations.

Was there a specific prediction? Was it written before the event? Was its deadline unambiguous? Was the event defined so that a disinterested observer could determine whether it happened? Were ordinary predictors and background event rates taken into account? Could the participant change the story afterward?

A report such as "I knew something would happen" is a legitimate description of a feeling.

It is not yet a prediction that can be scored.

The difference between the two is the scientific territory StorySpace intends to occupy.

## IV. Freezing tomorrow before tomorrow arrives

Consider a hypothetical example.

Someone reports a powerful sense that a particular team will win its next match. They can record the feeling alone—or choose to make a precise, time-bounded prediction.

For a testable forecast, they would need to name the match, specify an outcome and cutoff, assign a probability, and preserve the record before the result becomes known.

When the result is available, they can score it with the **Brier loss**, a standard way to evaluate a binary probability forecast:

\[
\mathrm{Brier}=(p-y)^2,
\]

where \(p\) is the declared probability and \(y\) is either zero or one for the outcome.

A person who assigns a probability of 0.8 to an event that occurs receives a loss of 0.04. If the event does not occur, the loss is 0.64. Lower is better.

But even that arithmetic is not a paranormal detector.

Forecasts must be compared with appropriate conventional predictions and independently established event rates. A 50–50 probability is not automatically a valid baseline for every kind of event. One impressive correct guess tells us very little about a person's long-run forecasting skill.

And there is a more fundamental concern: the timing of the prediction.

A browser can mark a record as "locally frozen," but browser storage and device clocks are editable. That is not an independently authenticated timestamp.

StorySpace says so plainly.

To make a genuine prospective evidentiary claim, a researcher would need to export the forecast and put it into independently verifiable custody before the outcome—for example, using a remotely recorded versioned commit or an appropriate trusted timestamp service.

This is a lesson Monolithic had recently encountered in its separate IST-FAM-001 physics audit: a protocol is strongest when its possible failure is defined and recorded **before** the outcome is examined.

StorySpace transfers that principle to temporal experience.

It does not assume that people can predict the future. It asks what would have to be written down for that proposition to receive a meaningful test.

## V. The quantum-eraser temptation

The project also includes a visual demonstration inspired by a different source of apparent retrocausal mystery: delayed-choice and quantum-eraser experiments.

In real quantum-optics experiments, researchers measure correlated particles and later compare selected subsets of their detection records. Depending on how those records are grouped, conditional patterns can appear that were not visible in an observer's unconditioned data.

The landmark delayed-choice quantum-eraser experiment reported by Yoon-Ho Kim and colleagues in 2000 helped make this issue famous.

The language of "erasing" which-path information and making a delayed "choice" can make it sound as though a later action changes an earlier detection event.

But the conventional operational account is more careful.

A later comparison of jointly recorded data can reveal correlations among subgroups without changing the earlier observer's marginal distribution. The appearance of a pattern after coincidence sorting is not, by itself, a usable method of communicating backward through time.

StorySpace makes this visible with a simple toy.

The interface constructs 2,520 simulated observations across 21 bins. The earlier aggregate distribution remains fixed. Users can then reveal the preassigned subgroup labels and observe how different patterns emerge in the subsets.

The original observations do not change.

The labels change what can be inferred from them.

The demonstration is intentionally **not** a quantum simulation. It does not model photons, perform an actual delayed-choice experiment, or settle the philosophical interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Its purpose is narrower: to make the distinction between **sorting evidence** and **rewriting an event** difficult to miss.

There is a human parallel.

A later event can cause us to retrieve and reorganize an earlier memory. The reorganized story may feel uncannily predictive.

That feeling does not establish that the earlier event itself has changed.

## VI. Recursion, but with a brake pedal

StorySpace also includes a recursive claim graph.

A user can build a larger explanation from smaller required claims: that a prediction was recorded early enough, that an outcome was independently verified, that a conventional comparator was properly specified, or that a particular statistical threshold was achieved.

Each subordinate requirement receives one of three familiar labels:

**PASS. FAIL. UNRESOLVED.**

Within the application's deliberately simple logic, a parent claim cannot pass unless all of its required children pass. A failed necessary child forces failure upward. Missing evidence leaves the parent unresolved.

The app checks for missing references and recursive cycles.

That makes it useful as an evidence-tracking aid, not as a mathematical theorem prover.

It cannot determine the truth of a physical explanation simply because someone marks a leaf as PASS.

Nor should it.

The point is to expose the places where confidence has outrun its prerequisites.

In the wider EFMW research environment, the same philosophy animates the project's adversarial verification collection, known as the Zoo: **a claim must be permitted to fail without requiring the research program to pretend that it succeeded.**

StorySpace gives that principle an unusually intimate application.

The claim under examination is sometimes not an equation.

It is the story a person tells about something they unmistakably felt.

## VII. What has actually been built

The public [StorySpace repository](https://github.com/enuminous/StorySpace) contains a static browser application, a JavaScript scoring and state-transition engine, automated tests, source documentation, and an initial experimental protocol designated **SS-001**.

The interface can record subjective experiences, save prospective forecasts, prevent ordinary interface edits after a local freeze, score self-reported binary outcomes, display the post-selection toy, and recursively summarize claim dependencies.

Its data remain in the local browser unless the user explicitly exports them. That is a privacy-conscious design choice, but it should not be confused with tamper-resistant research custody.

The repository's JavaScript controls have passed their automated checks. Those tests establish that the software's defined operations behave as expected under the tested conditions. They are not evidence of a human cognitive effect, and they do not validate a retrocausal physical mechanism.

The SS-001 protocol is likewise **proposed, not executed**.

Before it could support a confirmatory study, the program would need decisions about sample size, recruitment, independent outcome adjudication, realistic baselines, participant protections, selection bias, statistical power, multiple comparisons, and stopping rules. None of those requirements is satisfied merely by creating a website.

Nor has StorySpace demonstrated a new prediction advantage.

There is no published StorySpace participant dataset establishing precognition, no independent physical measurement of information arriving from the future, and no claimed experimental evidence for an alteration of the past.

It is an observatory and methodology scaffold: a place to keep the questions apart while designing a test that might eventually answer one of them.

## VIII. Why the word matters

The word *storyspace* is memorable because it refuses a false choice.

We do not have to dismiss a person's experience in order to challenge their explanation.

And we do not have to endorse an extraordinary physical interpretation in order to take the experience seriously.

People continually organize perception into narratives. They identify beginnings and endings, construct causes, infer intentions, recognize patterns, and connect present situations with remembered ones.

Often those abilities are useful. Sometimes they are misleading. At times, as in déjà vu, they can make an ordinary moment feel as though it has arrived from somewhere outside ordinary time.

The scientific question is not whether such moments feel strange enough to deserve attention.

They do.

The question is how to identify the source of the strangeness without allowing an explanation to borrow certainty from the feeling it is meant to explain.

That problem extends well beyond déjà vu.

It appears whenever a researcher notices an attractive mathematical pattern after examining the data. It appears when an institution writes its success story before checking its controls. It appears when artificial intelligence supplies a fluent explanation whose elegance is mistaken for independent evidence.

In each case, the story arrives with an almost irresistible sense that it belongs.

StorySpace asks us to detach it long enough to examine the seams.

## IX. The experiment that has not yet happened

Perhaps a future prospective study will show no unusual predictive skill in reports of déjà vu or apparent premonition.

That would be a useful result.

Perhaps it will identify circumstances in which people make better ordinary predictions than they consciously understand, drawing on weak cues, environmental patterns, or accumulated experience.

That would also be useful.

If a carefully controlled experiment ever produced a reliable predictive advantage that survived independent replication, researchers would still have to ask which mechanism explained it.

Better prediction does not automatically mean backward causation.

Nor should the word *retrocausality* be used as a catch-all for physical formalisms that are time-symmetric or for quantum correlations revealed by post-selection. Those are important topics in foundational physics, but their mathematical coherence is not evidence that human beings can receive messages from tomorrow.

The present project begins on the safer side of that boundary.

It does not announce an answer to the mystery of time.

It offers a method for preventing a compelling story from being mistaken for one.

That may be the most useful way to begin.

---

A familiar moment can still leave us astonished.

A dream can still feel prophetic.

An unexpected coincidence can still raise the hairs on our arms.

StorySpace does not try to confiscate any of that wonder.

It asks us to preserve the wonder accurately, so that we do not mistake the explanation added afterward for something that was measured beforehand.

The project's founding distinction is simple enough for a child to understand and difficult enough for entire research institutions to practice consistently:

**What happened? What did we think it meant? What evidence could tell us whether we were right?**

Those are three different questions.

StorySpace gives each one its own room.

And in a scientific culture that too often rewards the best explanation before asking whether it can fail, that separation may be worth more than another spectacular claim about the future.

*The Zoo abides. The story may be wrong.*
