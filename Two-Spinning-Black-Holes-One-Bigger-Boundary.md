# Two Spinning Black Holes, One Bigger Boundary

*A small paper for a very curious young reader*  
Prepared for Matthew Chenoweth Wright · 31 October 2026

Imagine two black holes dancing around each other.

Each one can spin while it dances, just as Earth spins while going around the Sun. Spinning and going around something are two different motions.

A settled, spinning black hole with no electric charge is described by a mathematical picture called **Kerr**, named after Roy Kerr. It can spin very fast, but this picture has a spin limit. “Hyperrotating” is not a special kind of black hole in our study. Here, we mean very fast-spinning, within that limit.

Each hole has an **event horizon**: a boundary beyond which nothing can send a message back to the faraway universe—not even light. It is not a hard shell. When scientists talk about its surface area, they mean the area of this boundary, not the dark patch in a telescope picture.

**First comes the dance.**

The holes move around one another. Their moving gravity sends ripples through space and time. These are gravitational waves. They carry away energy and some of the system's turning motion, called angular momentum. The orbit shrinks, and the holes circle faster. The waves usually rise in pitch, making a “chirp” when we turn the measurements into sound. [1]

A spinning hole also changes how nearby objects can move. Scientists call this **frame dragging**. Imagine nearby paths being swept around a spinning center, while remembering that space is not actually water in a whirlpool.

The directions of the spins matter. If the spins tilt away from the orbit's turning direction, the spins and the orbital plane can wobble. This is called **precession**. Different masses and spin directions change the dance and the waves it makes. [2]

**Then two become one.**

Near the collision, the tidy picture of two separate, settled Kerr holes stops being enough. Gravity changes violently. A common horizon forms around the merging system, and the final black hole starts out distorted.

There is no hard surface to smash like two stones. The changing spacetime outside the horizon sends out strong waves. Nothing needs to escape from inside the horizon to deliver those waves to us.

If the waves carry more momentum one way than another, the remaining hole can recoil the other way. It gets a **kick**, rather like a rocket moving opposite its exhaust. [3]

**Then comes the ringing.**

The distorted black hole settles toward a quieter, spinning Kerr shape. Its disturbances fade, rather like a struck bell becoming quiet. This stage is called **ringdown**.

It is not making ordinary sound in empty space. We measure gravitational waves. Their different “notes,” and how quickly those notes fade, help scientists work out the hole's mass and spin. [1]

**How can its boundary grow while energy escapes?**

Because mass and surface area are different things.

For Kerr holes with the same dimensionless spin, doubling the mass makes the horizon area four times as large. Changing the spin changes the area too: at the same mass, a faster-spinning Kerr hole has less horizon area.

So a merger can send energy away and leave a final mass smaller than the two original masses added together, while still making a horizon with more area than both original horizons combined.

Under the conditions of classical Einstein gravity, Hawking's area theorem says the total event-horizon area cannot decrease. An ordinary merger is expected to increase it. This rule has assumptions; quantum evaporation is a different situation. It is not a rule that every imaginable theory must obey. [4]

**Did we see the bigger boundary?**

We measured clues to it.

For the real event called **GW250114**, our repetition of the published data calculation gave a final area about **73% larger** than the two initial areas together. Under the original model, the central 90% range was about **41% to 116% larger**. The original observation was published by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA team in September 2025; we did not discover it. [4, 5]

Those two initial holes were inferred to have small spins. This event is therefore not an example of two nearly maximally spinning holes colliding. Fast spins belong to the broader story, not to a result our experiment established.

**Why check the ruler?**

Imagine guessing a bell's size from its ringing. You need the right rule connecting the sound to the bell.

We made pretend ringdown signals and changed them in a carefully chosen way. The ordinary calculation could match the changed ringing beautifully, yet give the wrong mass and area. Some pretend growing systems looked as though their area had shrunk.

We also found that some uncertainty ranges missed the known pretend answer too often. We recorded that failure rather than hiding it. These were simplified tests of the measuring method—not full simulations of colliding holes, and not evidence that the real measurement was wrong. [5]

**Where does EFMW belong?**

EFMW helped organize our questions: What did we assume? What did we measure? Which rule connects them? What could fool that rule?

Its proposed C1 equations also suggest checking whether horizon area alone is enough to calculate a quantity called **entropy**, which is used in black-hole thermodynamics. Under a specific restriction, an extra field-dependent factor enters that calculation. We still need actual black-hole solutions and predicted waves before this becomes a physical EFMW test. [5]

The holes can dance, merge, ring, and leave a bigger boundary. Our job is to listen carefully—and check that our mathematical ruler measures what we think it measures.
