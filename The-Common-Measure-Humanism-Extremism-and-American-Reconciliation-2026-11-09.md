# The Common Measure
### Humanism, Extremism, and the Possibility of American Reconciliation

*An essay on liberalism, conservatism, and the moral foundations of democratic coexistence*

**Matthew Chenoweth Wright · November 9, 2026**

---

## I. The Mirror

America has developed a peculiar political symmetry.

Liberals increasingly describe conservatives as extremists. Conservatives increasingly describe liberals in precisely the same terms. Each side believes the other threatens the country's fundamental character, and each regards its own position as a necessary defense against that threat.

In October 2025, the Pew Research Center found that 78 percent of Democrats regarded the Republican Party as too extreme, while 77 percent of Republicans said the same of the Democratic Party.

This symmetry is striking. But it presents a philosophical problem.

If two people accuse one another of extremism, the accusations cannot be evaluated merely by counting them. Their agreement that the other is dangerous tells us something about their mutual alienation, but nothing conclusive about the merits of either accusation.

We require a common measure.

Without one, political language becomes circular: I am reasonable because I oppose your extremism, and you are extremist because you oppose my reasonableness.

Democratic society cannot survive indefinitely inside such a circle.

## II. Liberalism Is Not Inherently an Extreme

The proposition that liberalism is inherently extremist deserves examination because it reveals a confusion between political position and moral principle.

Liberalism, in its broad philosophical tradition, begins with the moral significance of the individual human being.

It holds that human beings possess rights that governments must respect; that conscience should not be subject to arbitrary authority; that law should apply equally; that political legitimacy requires public accountability; and that power must justify itself before those over whom it is exercised.

Modern social liberalism extends these principles into questions of practical opportunity, arguing that freedom can become hollow when poverty, exclusion, or preventable deprivation make its exercise impossible.

These propositions may be contested in their interpretation, extent, and implementation. But they are not inherently extremist.

They belong to a tradition that helped establish constitutional democracy.

Indeed, certain liberal principles are so deeply embedded in American political life that many conservatives defend them as vigorously as liberals.

Freedom of religion, freedom of speech, property rights, due process, constitutional limitations, and protection against arbitrary government power all draw upon liberal traditions.

The American conservative who defends these principles is not thereby abandoning conservatism. Nor is the American liberal who defends equality necessarily rejecting tradition.

Their philosophical inheritances overlap.

Here lies our first opportunity for rapprochement:

**The principles that permit us to disagree politically are often principles we already share.**

## III. Conservatism Is Not Inherently an Extreme Either

An honest humanist argument must extend the same intellectual charity to conservatism.

Conservatism, at its most thoughtful, begins with an appreciation of human fallibility.

It asks whether institutions embody accumulated experience that reformers may insufficiently understand. It recognizes that social arrangements can possess value beyond their immediate efficiency, and that rapid transformation can impose costs upon people least equipped to bear them.

Its concerns include continuity, responsibility, family, community, local autonomy, institutional stability, and the preservation of inherited liberties.

These concerns are not inherently hostile to human dignity.

They can arise from a profoundly humane understanding of human vulnerability.

A liberal may ask why an institution has not changed.

A conservative may ask what will be lost if it does.

Both questions deserve answers.

Sometimes the institution perpetuates an injustice and must be transformed. Sometimes its removal would destroy relationships or protections that are difficult to reconstruct. Sometimes the proper response combines reform with preservation.

This is not an argument that every tradition deserves survival, or that every reform constitutes progress.

It is an argument that human beings possess legitimate reasons for fearing different kinds of harm.

**One fears the injustice of remaining where we are. The other fears the damage of moving without sufficient care.**

Neither fear requires contempt for the other.

## IV. The Difference Between Radicalism and Extremism

We must therefore distinguish two concepts that American political language frequently conflates.

Radicalism concerns the extent of proposed change.

Extremism, for purposes of democratic humanism, concerns the principles or methods by which political objectives are pursued.

A movement may propose sweeping economic reform while respecting constitutional democracy, political pluralism, and equal human dignity.

Another may advocate relatively modest institutional changes while endorsing political violence, disenfranchisement, or arbitrary punishment of its opponents.

The first may be radical without being extremist.

The second may be extremist without being especially radical.

Extremism is therefore not adequately measured by distance from the prevailing political center.

A political center is a description of where public opinion happens to congregate. Its position can change across generations, sometimes profoundly.

There have been societies in which the prevailing consensus defended slavery, denied women political participation, or imposed religious conformity.

A moral principle does not become extremist merely because it contradicts a society's existing arrangements.

Nor does a harmful practice become humane because it commands a majority.

The critical distinction is between challenging the existing distribution of power and denying the equal moral standing of human beings.

**Humanism is not the midpoint between dignity and indignity. It is the commitment to dignity by which competing political proposals can be judged.**

## V. A Standard Neither Side Owns

If this principle is to help reconcile Americans, it cannot belong exclusively to one political party.

Humanism must remain available to anyone prepared to practice it.

A liberal who defends free expression only for political allies has departed from a liberal principle.

A conservative who defends constitutional limits only when opponents hold office has departed from a conservative principle.

A progressive who dismisses the suffering of rural communities because of their voting patterns fails the test of human solidarity.

A traditionalist who treats minority citizens as less entitled to equal protection fails that same test.

These failures are not necessarily identical in severity, consequence, or frequency. Each must be evaluated through evidence.

But the underlying measure must be consistent.

We can articulate a civic minimum:

Every person possesses equal human dignity.

Every citizen is entitled to equal protection under law.

Political opponents retain constitutional rights.

Public power remains accountable to lawful institutions.

Elections must be administered fairly and their lawful outcomes respected.

Political violence, intimidation, and collective punishment are unacceptable methods of ordinary democratic competition.

Claims about public affairs must remain open to evidence, correction, and scrutiny.

These principles do not resolve disagreements over taxation, immigration, healthcare, environmental regulation, education, or the proper size of government.

Nor should they.

Democracy exists partly because reasonable people can disagree about difficult questions without becoming enemies.

The civic minimum establishes the conditions under which such disagreement remains possible.

It does not dictate its conclusions.

## VI. Reconciliation Without False Equivalence

Rapprochement is not surrender.

Nor does reconciliation require the injured to describe their injuries as misunderstandings.

There are real differences between political movements. There are real threats to constitutional government, real forms of discrimination, and real abuses of institutional power.

They must be investigated and addressed.

It would be a mistake to assume that whenever two sides oppose one another, truth must lie halfway between them.

But it would be equally mistaken to assume that every person who votes for a political movement endorses every action undertaken in its name.

Individuals are not interchangeable with parties, parties are not interchangeable with leaders, and leaders' declarations are not interchangeable with their conduct.

These distinctions matter.

We can hold political officials accountable without treating millions of fellow citizens as irredeemable.

We can reject an argument without humiliating the person who advances it.

We can recognize that someone has been misled without concluding that they are incapable of learning.

And we can defend the rights of people who would not necessarily return the courtesy, without surrendering our obligation to defend those rights.

The purpose of reconciliation is not to abolish accountability.

**It is to make accountability possible without requiring permanent social enmity.**

## VII. The Human Being Across the Table

Imagine two Americans seated across from one another.

One identifies as liberal. The other identifies as conservative.

The liberal worries that political power may be used to exclude vulnerable people from the protections and opportunities of citizenship.

The conservative worries that distant institutions may impose sweeping changes without understanding the communities affected.

Both may have legitimate concerns.

Both may also hold beliefs that require correction.

The conversation becomes more productive when neither begins by assigning the other a moral diagnosis.

Instead, each asks:

*What are you afraid of losing?*

The liberal may answer: equality, freedom, security, or the assurance that a child will not be denied opportunity because of who they are.

The conservative may answer: autonomy, continuity, livelihood, community, or the assurance that cherished commitments will not be dismissed without consideration.

These answers do not eliminate policy disagreement.

But they identify the human interests beneath it.

A society capable of hearing those interests can ask a better question:

*Can we protect what matters to you without sacrificing what matters to me?*

Sometimes the answer will be yes.

Sometimes the demands will conflict, and the law must make difficult distinctions.

But even then, the other person remains a participant in a common civic undertaking.

That recognition is the beginning of reconciliation.

## VIII. The Discipline of Intellectual Humility

There is another requirement, perhaps the most difficult.

We must recover the distinction between conviction and certainty.

It is possible to believe deeply in a political principle while acknowledging uncertainty about how best to implement it.

It is possible to regard an opposing argument as mistaken while recognizing that it contains an insight one has overlooked.

And it is possible to change one's mind without experiencing the change as humiliation.

A healthy democratic conversation should allow someone to say:

*I was wrong about that.*

Those five words should be regarded as evidence of intellectual integrity rather than weakness.

The same principle applies to institutions, journalism, scholarship, and political leadership.

A claim that cannot be questioned is no longer simply a claim. It has become an instrument of authority.

A movement incapable of correcting itself risks transforming loyalty into an alternative to truth.

A democracy requires something better: citizens prepared to examine not only the errors of their opponents, but also the assumptions of their own side.

Reconciliation begins when correction ceases to be understood as capitulation.

## IX. The Invitation

To the American conservative:

You need not abandon your concern for tradition, local community, individual responsibility, or constitutional restraint to recognize the equal dignity of people whose lives differ from yours.

Indeed, those commitments can provide powerful reasons to defend that dignity.

To the American liberal:

You need not abandon your concern for equality, inclusion, social protection, or reform to recognize the sincerity of people who fear institutional overreach or the unintended consequences of rapid change.

Indeed, your commitment to pluralism provides a powerful reason to hear them.

To both:

You need not agree about the future of the country to acknowledge that you must inhabit it together.

You do not have to like every argument.

You do not have to excuse misconduct.

You do not have to relinquish a single well-supported fact.

But you can refuse to transform disagreement into dehumanization.

You can demand evidence rather than loyalty.

You can distinguish reform from destruction, caution from cruelty, and disagreement from disloyalty.

You can grant your opponent the right to participate in the political process even while working vigorously to defeat their proposals.

And you can remember that the purpose of democratic institutions is not to guarantee that one faction will prevail forever.

It is to make peaceful political competition possible without depriving the losing side of its place in society.

## X. A Common Future

America's deepest division may not be between liberalism and conservatism.

It may be between two ways of understanding political life.

One treats politics as an existential struggle in which the other side's continued participation constitutes a threat.

The other treats politics as an imperfect, sometimes painful process through which people with competing interests and convictions seek to govern themselves without violence or domination.

The first demands victory without reconciliation.

The second recognizes that political victory is temporary, while coexistence must endure.

Humanism belongs to the second tradition.

It does not erase the moral distinctions between political actions. It does not insist that all perspectives are equally sound. It does not require tolerance of violence or abandonment of the vulnerable.

It asks only that our political judgments remain answerable to something larger than factional allegiance: the rights, dignity, and lived reality of human beings.

Liberalism can contribute its insistence upon liberty, equality, and reform.

Conservatism can contribute its regard for continuity, prudence, and the limits of political power.

Neither tradition is guaranteed to honor its best principles.

Both are capable of doing so.

Their meeting place is not an artificial midpoint between left and right.

It is a shared civic commitment that allows serious disagreement without the destruction of mutual legitimacy.

**We do not have to become politically alike to become humanly responsible to one another.**

That is the possibility of rapprochement.

Not agreement without difference.

Not forgiveness without accountability.

Not unity imposed by authority.

But a society in which disagreement no longer requires the invention of an enemy.

And perhaps, in that recognition, Americans may rediscover something they did not truly lose:

**A country does not belong to whichever side wins the argument. It belongs to all the people who must live with the answer.**
