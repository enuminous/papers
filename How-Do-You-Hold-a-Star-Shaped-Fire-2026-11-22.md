# How Do You Hold a Star-Shaped Fire?

*What a small computational tokamak project is learning about magnetic boundaries, unstable plasmas, and the difference between a good simulation and a power plant*

**Matthew Chenoweth Wright · Monolithic LLC**  
November 22, 2026

A simulated plasma starts to slip. In one calculation, the disturbance grows at 808 per second. In another, it grows at 628 per second. Both calculations come from the same software and start from a reconstruction of the same tokamak. If you were designing a control system around those numbers, which one would you believe?

That was the question behind the latest round of our Tokamak project. We began with a broader curiosity about the boundary between confinement and escape. It led us to reproduce a published equilibrium, move coils in a simulation, trace heat toward a wall, and finally ask whether two ways of calculating an instability actually described the same physics. The comparison proved especially revealing because the plasma still slipped after we got the calculations to agree. [OPENSTEP-007 benchmark](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/0cb88b21e8fce6106aa3b7d352a7a7dbfc0b22b7/benchmarks/openstep-007)

## The boundary that does two jobs

A tokamak uses magnetic fields to confine a hot, electrically charged gas called plasma. The field lines wind around a ring-shaped chamber, and in a cross-section you can picture many of the magnetic surfaces as nested loops. Follow a field line on one of those surfaces, and it winds around the machine while staying on its surface. At the edge of the confined region, the pattern changes. The **separatrix** divides the closed magnetic surfaces from regions where field lines can lead to material targets. In the cross-section it can pass through a crossing called an **X-point**.

This geometry lets a tokamak do two things that pull in opposite directions. It can keep much of the hot plasma away from the walls while providing a route for exhaust to reach the **divertor**, a component built to receive heat and particles. Heat and particles can cross the edge through transport processes, and the shape of the field helps determine where they go from there. A magnetic diagram can look nearly identical after a small coil adjustment while the hottest patch on a target moves or changes intensity. [ITER lecture on magnetic transport](https://www.iter.org/sites/default/files/media/2026-02/ricci_lec.pdf)

Our starting analogy came from A. Beth Jones’s TriSphere blueprint, which prompted us to ask how an interior, a boundary, and an exhaust route might be modeled together. We built a simplified accounting model and compared its thirteen-layer and five-structure versions. Both passed their accounting checks. Their driven radial motions differed by more than the comparison allowed. That gave us a reason to test the analogy in pieces. In a separate analytic plasma model, we imposed an asymmetric change in magnetic flux and watched the X-point approach the magnetic axis until the two merged. That calculation illustrates how a confinement boundary can disappear in a particular model. Checking the proposed 4:1 midplane relation would require defining exactly what is being measured and testing it with a free-boundary coil calculation. [TriSphere results](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/blob/main/TRISPHERE-RESULTS-2026-11-20.md) · [Asymmetric X-point calculation](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/blob/main/X-POINT-CONTINUATION.md)

## Move a coil, follow the heat

For a more realistic magnetic test, we started from a published equilibrium for a historical scenario of the UK’s STEP spherical tokamak program. An equilibrium is a calculated balance of forces for a given plasma and magnetic configuration. We reproduced the reference magnetic flux in the core to about **0.15% root-mean-square error**. That gave us a baseline from which to move a coil and ask how the magnetic field and the predicted heat footprint changed. [UKAEA’s STEP plasma study](https://scientific-publications.ukaea.uk/papers/flat-top-plasma-operational-space-of-the-step-power-plant/) · [Equilibrium replay](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/main/benchmarks/openstep-001)

In one scan, a **−1 cm** shift of the p9 coil reduced the modeled peak on a sharp target from about **13.7 to 11.8 megawatts per square metre**. A shift in the opposite direction raised it to about **22.0**. These estimates used the same assumptions about how widely the heat spread and how much was radiated away before reaching the target. The shape of the target itself mattered, too. Giving the modeled corner a small radius changed some peak estimates substantially. No geometry in the earlier coil scan met every screening criterion. The changes tell us where to investigate heat loading and why a lower value at one operating point is only one part of the decision. [Coil scan](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/main/benchmarks/openstep-003) · [Heat-footprint study](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/main/benchmarks/openstep-005)

## Why the plasma was slipping at two different speeds

The next question was how a small vertical displacement grows. For this we used a published computational case from DIII-D, a separate tokamak with a publicly available reference model for the plasma and conducting vessel. One calculation estimates how fast a particular disturbance should grow when it is still small. The other puts a small version of that disturbance into the simulation and advances it through time. The two calculations should give comparable rates if they describe the same evolution in that small-disturbance regime.

We traced a difference to how the software handles plasma-current targets. With those targets enabled, the time simulation rescales the pressure and magnetic-field profiles to meet them. The eigenvalue calculation holds the profile amplitudes fixed. In OPENSTEP-007, we held the amplitudes fixed in both methods and included the response of the moving flux bounds in the eigenvalue calculation. We also ran disturbances with opposite signs and took half the difference between their trajectories, which reduces the influence of drift shared by both.

With those settings the finer time simulation gave **813.73 per second**, against **807.70 per second** from the eigenvalue calculation. The **0.75%** gap was within the benchmark’s **10%** criterion. A coarser timestep and a tenfold smaller starting disturbance passed as well. The comparable runs with current targets enabled still differed by roughly **22–24%**. They need an eigenvalue calculation that includes the response to those targets before we can judge their time evolution on the same terms. The agreement we obtained is computational and applies to the fixed-profile case on the archived coarse mesh. It still describes a growing disturbance. [OPENSTEP-007 methods and results](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/0cb88b21e8fce6106aa3b7d352a7a7dbfc0b22b7/benchmarks/openstep-007)

## The distance from a plasma to a power plant

An earlier simulation made the urgency of control concrete. An idealized controller using prescribed coil currents suppressed a small displacement when its measurement arrived instantly. It failed the same suppression criterion with delays of half a millisecond and one millisecond. For STEP, the vessel, electrical circuits, sensors, and power supplies would all matter. We still lack the STEP passive-conductor and controller data for that calculation. [OPENSTEP-006 stability and control study](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/main/benchmarks/openstep-006)

A power plant adds more questions. Can it remove exhaust heat continuously? Can its fuel cycle replace the tritium consumed? Can it turn enough heat into electricity to cover its own heating, pumping, and other loads? In our reduced STEP scenarios, changing assumptions about efficiency, heat recovery, and fixed electrical loads took the modeled plant from net electricity consumption to net production. A single promising plasma calculation leaves all those questions open. [OPENSTEP-006 reactor accounting](https://github.com/enuminous/Tokamak/tree/main/benchmarks/openstep-006) · [ITER on fusion gain](https://www.iter.org/faqs?thematic=68)

What we’re attempting is a computational test bench where each claim can be checked at the scale where it is made. Magnetic geometry can be tested against a reference equilibrium. A heat footprint can be tested against changes in tracing method and target geometry. A growth rate can be tested against a time simulation with the same assumptions. The next control calculation has a concrete question to answer. How fast does the displacement grow when both methods let the plasma profiles respond to the same current targets? Getting that answer would tell us which instability a proposed controller actually has to catch.
